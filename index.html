LeHami: Symplectic Latent Dynamics for World Models

Romina Yalovetzky1,*, Randall Balestriero2, Yann LeCun1,3

1Courant Institute, New York University  ·  2Brown University  ·  3AMI Labs

*Corresponding author: romina.yalovetzky@nyu.edu

Code Paper (arXiv) · coming soon

The problem Joint-embedding predictive architectures (JEPAs) learn world models directly in latent space, but unconstrained autoregressive predictors may contract the latent distribution over long rollouts.

The method We introduce Symplectic Hamiltonian JEPA (LeHami), which replaces the standard predictor with an action-conditioned Hamiltonian flow over a structured latent state (q, p) integrated using an exactly symplectic leapfrog step. We obtain the momentum-like component from temporal derivatives of the learned position-like representation, providing an explicit second-order state for prediction and control.

The guarantee This construction preserves phase-space volume at every rollout step and rules out uniform contraction, asymptotically stable fixed points, and merging of distinct latent states, independently of the learned parameters or action sequence.

The results On conservative and actuated visual pendulum environments, LeHami substantially improves long-horizon velocity prediction over an unconstrained JEPA world model and empirically maintains constant latent volume where the baseline contracts. The learned flow also supports accurate integration at unseen sub-frame step sizes and numerical time reversal. These results show how geometric structure can provide inference-time guarantees that are complementary to representation regularization in latent world models.

TL;DR  Give a JEPA's latent space the structure of a physical phase space. LeHami predicts with a Hamiltonian flow over (q, p), so latent volume is preserved by construction, long-horizon velocity prediction improves, and the model can be integrated at unseen step sizes and run backward in time. It also plans with a smaller and cheaper predictor, though it does not yet match the best unconstrained baseline's goal-reaching success.

The idea, from the intuition

Think of a pendulum. Its angle alone does not tell you what happens next: you also need how fast it is swinging. Physics bundles the two into a phase space, a position-like coordinate q and a momentum-like coordinate p, and the motion is a smooth flow through that space along the level sets of an energy function H.

A standard JEPA world model has no such structure. It encodes a frame into a vector and an unconstrained network predicts the next vector. Nothing stops that network from squeezing the latent space a little at every step; over a long rollout the squeeze compounds, distinct states drift together and the information that told them apart is gone.

LeHami gives the latent space the phase-space structure directly. The encoder maps each frame to a point (q, p), and the predictor is a learned Hamiltonian Hφ = Kφ(p) + Vφ(q, u) advanced by a leapfrog step. The action u enters through the potential, so a torque literally tilts the energy landscape the state moves on.

Three consequences of the structure

1 · Nothing collapses

A symplectic step can stretch and shear a cloud of latent states but never shrinks its volume. So the flow cannot contract uniformly, cannot have an asymptotically stable fixed point, and cannot merge two distinct states, whatever the learned parameters or the actions.

2 · A curve, not a lookup

The predictor describes a continuous flow, so the step size h is a free number. The model was trained on frames spaced h apart but can be integrated at h/2 or h/4 to read off states between the frames it saw.

3 · Run it backward

Because each step is invertible, applying the same actions in reverse order retraces the forward trajectory. This is what makes numerical time reversal possible, and it is a sanity check that the model learned a reversible flow rather than a lossy map.

A learned energy that tracks the real one

The Hamiltonian is learned from pixels alone, yet along a rollout it stays flat, and the level it sits at follows the true energy of the pendulum. Each of the four pendulums below swings with a different energy; the learned Hφ of each is almost constant over 96 rollout steps.

Architecture

LeHami keeps a JEPA's encoder and training signal and swaps only the predictor. Two pieces are new: a fixed stencil that reads the momentum off the encoder trajectory, and the Hamiltonian flow that replaces the transformer predictor.

  1. Encode. A pixel encoder maps a frame (or a short window of frames) to the position-like embedding qt.
  2. Read off the momentum. A zero-parameter Savitzky–Golay stencil differentiates the last few q's to give pt = dq/dt, an explicit second-order state.
  3. Flow. The Hamiltonian flow applies K leapfrog steps with the actions ut, …, ut+K−1.
  4. Train. Predictions are compared with the encoded future frames, with a regularizer (e.g. SIGReg) on the embeddings to prevent encoder collapse.

Two pendulum environments

We test on pixel observations (64×64 RGB) of a pendulum. The conservative environment has three rod lengths and no actuation. The actuated one adds three bob masses and a piecewise-constant torque.

Results

Long-horizon prediction and latent volume

On the conservative pendulum (three rod lengths, three seeds per model), LeHami predicts angle and angular velocity more accurately than the unconstrained LeWM baseline over long rollouts. The velocity error of LeWM is about 2.8× larger over the first ten rollout steps. The right panel tracks the cumulative log-volume of the one-step map: it stays at zero for LeHami and falls steadily for LeWM.

The animation shows where that curve comes from. A unit disc of latent perturbations is pushed through the one-step map at each rollout step. The ellipse axes are the largest and smallest singular values of the map's Jacobian, averaged over 45 test episodes. For LeHami the product of the two is 1: the disc is stretched one way and squeezed the other, with its area preserved. For LeWM the smallest singular value is essentially zero, so the map flattens the disc onto a line at every step.

Unseen step sizes and time reversal

Because the learned flow is a continuous-time Hamiltonian system, it can be integrated with step sizes it never saw in training. Halving or quartering the trained step leaves the error within about 1.3× of the trained step, whereas doubling it is clearly worse. Reversing the flow and integrating back returns to the starting state with a round-trip error below 10−4 % for up to 30 steps.

Planning on the actuated pendulum

We plan at inference time by searching for an action sequence that brings the latent prediction at the horizon to the latent of a goal frame, using the cross-entropy method with model-predictive control. Goals are 30 steps ahead and contain a position clause, a velocity clause, or both. The budget is capped at 15 TFLOP per episode, so each model trades the cost of one solve against how often it replans.

Both models decode the rod's mass and length along the whole rollout, so differences in planning do not come from failed system identification. What differs is how velocity enters the latent:

  • LeWM, one frame (W=1): the per-frame goal embedding carries no velocity, so it fails the velocity clause.
  • LeWM, window with tied weights: captures speed but is blind to direction, since it maps ±ω to the same latent. 92 % of the goals need the correct sign.
  • LeWM, window with untied weights: breaks time symmetry, captures signed velocity and reaches the highest success, at a high cost per solve that forces it to replan less often.
  • LeHami: signed velocity is guaranteed by the structure, since p is read off the encoder trajectory with no extra parameters, and the Hamiltonian flow has 2.23M parameters against 3.31M for LeWM's transformer and projection.

The honest summary: LeHami does not beat the best unconstrained baseline on success. LeWM with a three-frame untied window reaches 0.800 on velocity goals against 0.527, and the single-frame LeWM is best on position. What LeHami offers is a cheaper way to get signed velocity: a predictor with fewer parameters, less compute per episode (8.05 against 12.87 TFLOP on position goals at the same replanning interval), and the volume-preserving guarantees. The paper points to a jointly learned momentum embedding in place of the fixed stencil as the route to closing the gap.

Code and reproducing the paper
